Machine Learning Module

This page documents my learning journey through the Machine Learning module at the University of Essex, as part of the Master’s Degree in Artificial Intelligence.

It is structured as a living journal: each unit captures what was studied (core content), the artefacts produced, and my general impressions. Entries will be added progressively as the module unfolds.

Keywords: machine learning, University of Essex, AI Master’s, learning journal, units & artefacts.

Reflective Journal

In the first unit, I explored the disruptive impact of Industry 4.0 and 5.0 across industries (Metcalf, 2024). It prompted me to reflect deep on how technology is reshaping the labour market and the key skills needed to thrive in this new environment (WEF, 2025). While these changes concern me due to the risk of job displacement and greater inequality, I am also optimistic that they may create more space for creativity and more meaningful work. Through collaborative discussions and peer reviews, I was able to expand and contextualise my understanding through practical examples across different industries. These cases highlighted the critical consequences of information system failures, particularly in machine learning, while also providing insights into common pitfalls and approaches for designing more robust systems.

The subsequent stages deepened my understanding of the machine learning process, beginning with Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)(Gerón, 2025). Through the practical exercise, I developed a broader analytical toolkit and learned to select and combine techniques according to the patterns emerging from the data. I assessed data quality through missing values and duplicates, examined distributions and anomalies using descriptive statistics and univariate visualisations, and explored relationships through multivariate analysis and correlations (Harmadi, 2021; Salmanuddin et al., 2023). This process also encouraged me to extend the initial analysis with alternative visualisations and interpretations, reinforcing EDA as an iterative process that informs subsequent data preparation decisions.

I also developed a better understanding of correlation and regression in unit 3(Géron, 2025), although I initially struggled to identify which correlation method was appropriate for different contexts, particularly Pearson versus Spearman. The professor's explanations during the seminars helped me clarify their differences and understand when to apply each method.

Units 4, 5 and 6 introduced clustering and linear regression, with a stronger emphasis on group work, which was a rewarding experience that strengthned my collaboration and leadership skills. I found it particularly challenging to coordinate across different time zones, cultures and professional backgrounds, especially given the ambitious project scope and the limited time available before submission. Within the group, my main contribution was bringing clarity and structure to the project by defining tasks, supporting the selection of management tools, communicating through visual summaries and keeping the team focused on priorities and deadlines. I was also responsible for EDA, reviewing the methodology and findings, investigating limitations and developing the study's conclusions. Although I believe we did a good job and I was proud of the final result, However, I recognised that earlier methodological alignment and clearer expectations could have reduced rework and improved collaboration. I also learned to balance my enthusiasm for deeper exploration with project constraints. Moving forward, I intend to develop more culturally adaptable communication through active feedback (Crowne, 2026) and strengthen planning through shared objectives, early alignment and regular checkpoints (Liu et al., 2025; Luciano et al., 2025).

Units 7–10 were intellectually exciting but also overwhelming, introducing more complex topics such as Artificial Neural Networks, Convolutional Neural Networks and Natural Language Processing. Initially, I felt lost when approaching the individual assignment, faced with countless research directions and methodological choices under tight time constraints. This challenge pushed me to develop more focused research skills, discipline and critical thinking, gradually building confidence in making evidence-based decisions. I chose to explore computer vision in healthcare, comparing traditional machine learning with deep learning approaches. Rather than simply pursuing model performance, I wanted to understand the experimental process and the trade-offs behind methodological choices. Freezing the pretrained backbone allowed me to focus on specific components while managing the complexity of deep learning. During a seminar, I shared my surprise at the number of parameters and experimental possibilities. The tutor explained that navigating these decisions requires time and experience, reinforcing my motivation to experiment, learn systematically and begin developing that expertise myself.The recorded presentation format also challenged me to strengthen my communication skills, particularly my ability to synthesise complex information, build a compelling narrative and speak confidently. Although demanding, I valued this opportunity to develop skills that are essential not only in professional settings but also in everyday life.

I came to appreciate how multidimensional AI deployment truly is, requiring expertise across different disciplines. What struck me most was realising that a model's journey does not end at deployment. Continuous monitoring, adjustments and a solid understanding of infrastructure are essential to maintaining reliable systems, addressing emerging biases and ensuring models remain effective as conditions evolve(Sendas e Rajales, 2024; Shrivastava et al., 2025).
